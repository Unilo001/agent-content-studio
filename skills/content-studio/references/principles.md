# Principles with mechanism (lookup)

Read when writing a brief or script, when reviewing a beat and you must name *which* principle it breaks and what it costs the viewer, or when two principles pull in different directions.

Use principles as a way of thinking, not a template. "Hook, three points, CTA" produces posts that look right and explain nothing. If you cannot state the mechanism in your own words, you copied a step. Hard rules in BRAND.md (honesty, consent, banned claims) always beat a principle.

## General content rules

1. **One point, shown before the next.** Viewers have limited capacity; remove the interesting but irrelevant. Test: the point in one sentence. Exception: when the series itself is the point, explain the structure in the first beat.
2. **Tempo is motion, not more cards.** Long static holds read as boring; stacked cards read as rushed. Fix both with movement inside the frame and full read time for text. Picture can change every 1.5-3 s; text follows read time. Slow motion lowers perceived activation, so do not use it as "tension". Exception: silence for 0.3-0.75 s right before the payoff is correct.
3. **The knowledge gap must be closable, and the viewer must know enough.** Curiosity needs a focused gap plus a knowledge base. Show the answer first and hold back only the *why*. Never use a pronoun with no referent as the gap.
4. **Open on the event, not the introduction.** Skip decisions happen in the first ~3 s. Rider/subject already moving beats title, logo, mockup, collage or parallax. Exception: build-in-public pieces where a real shot is frame 0 and the drawing comes after.
5. **Write for the person who does not know.** The curse of knowledge makes every insider cut look obvious. Put the product card or the one-line explanation early.
6. **"But" / "therefore", never "and then".** A chain of facts is a list; a chain of tension is a story. Exception: a deliberate list.
7. **Concrete beats general, never invented.** Two numbers in contrast beat one. If a number cannot be sourced, be vague rather than false.
8. **Name the misconception aloud and let the viewer guess first.** Prediction before demonstration improves understanding (moderate effect; do not promise more). Guess about a scenario, never about the person on screen.
9. **End on the point and let it stand.** Payoff and ending get time; no new idea after payoff; end card after the point. Exception: deliberate loops.
10. **Give a reason to send.** Watch time, likes and sends count most. An answer key, a surprise or something useful; the ask lives in the caption, not on screen. Never "send this to a friend".
11. **Vary the form, keep the identity.** Followers tire of one template. Vary technique, camera, cut language, layer count, timing, type motion; keep logo, labelling, tone. A new technique should change at least 3 of those 5 axes.
12. **Honesty is part of the mechanism.** The hook is true for the content; illustration is labelled inside the graphic; unfinished products are described as unfinished. One neutral label per piece, not a stack of disclaimers.
13. **Name first, then mechanism; tie analogies to the real product.** Pre-training the term improves transfer. An analogy that does not match how the product works teaches the wrong thing.
14. **Text must be readable; sound and text do not do the same job twice.** Plan for a share of viewers without sound: text and audio must each work alone. Same words in speech and on screen weaken transfer. With voice-over, on-screen text is <= 3 words or one number per card.

## When principles conflict
- Tempo (2) vs one point (1): cut a card, do not speed up.
- Event first (4) vs prior knowledge (5): event first, then give the viewer the word for what they see within ~3 s.
- Variety (11) vs recognisability: vary technique, keep the identity.
- Mystery (3) vs understanding (5): hold back only the *why*, in beats under ~5 s; A/B test.
- Questions in hooks: evidence is mixed; test.

## Ten questions before making a post
Run on the script (story + on-screen text + VO) before building, and again before render if anything changed. One "no" = rewrite the beat. Never render "to see if it works".

1. Can someone who has not seen the brief state the point in one sentence after reading the script?
2. Is there one idea, and is each claim shown on screen before the next arrives?
3. Is frame 0 an event happening now (not a title, logo or slow reveal)?
4. Does a stranger know what the product is, and what "it / the box / the number" refers to, within ~3 s - in words, not only a small label?
5. Can *this* piece close the gap the hook opens, and does it close before ~10-15 s (or partly before ~8 s)?
6. Does "and then" appear between any beats? Rewrite as "but / therefore".
7. Is every number sourced, labelled as illustration/computed where needed, and never "tested"?
8. Does every text card have read time (see cs-story), and is the same wording not spoken and shown at once?
9. Do payoff and ending get >= ~2 s without new ideas, with logo/CTA after the point?
10. Does the form differ from the last three posts (technique, ending, length), and is there a reason to send without saying "send this"?

## Cold test
Questions 1 and 4 need a reader who has not seen the brief. Recognition is not explanation: a cheap model passing a piece the human rejected proves this. Ask the tester three things in their own words - "What was it about? What is the product? What should I do?" If they only repeat the on-screen text, it fails.
